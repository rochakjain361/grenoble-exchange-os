§ — Three climates, one bag

You are packing for a 25°C spread

Most exchange students pack for one winter. You are packing for Vienna in September at 22°C, Iceland in early October at 3°C with gale-force wind, and the French Alps in December below freezing — and Iceland comes before you ever reach your flat, so nothing can be deferred to a shop in Grenoble.

Allowance out
35 kg

Split across two pieces, 32 kg max each.

Allowance back
40 kg

Five more than you left with. Don't hoard capacity.

11.8 kg

Roughly a third of the bag. See the list.

Coldest day
≈ -1°C

Grenoble in December. Iceland's wind will feel worse.

The three rules that decide everything else

  • Layers beat bulk. Base + fleece + shell handles every temperature on this trip and packs into a third of the volume of one heavy coat. It also adapts — a single thick jacket is useless in Barcelona in November.
  • You have a washing machine. Pack for ten days, not 103. This is the mistake that costs people five kilos, and those five kilos are worth roughly €400 as Iceland food.
  • Cotton is the wrong fabric for Iceland. Wet cotton in wind stops insulating entirely and gets genuinely dangerous on an exposed Ring Road stop. Base layers must be merino or synthetic. Two merino pieces also go three or four days without smelling — which on a three-night trip with one backpack is worth more than the price difference.

Buy in Grenoble, not Bangalore

The heavy winter coat, proper gloves, a warmer beanie and snow boots. Grenoble is an alpine city — Decathlon and the second-hand ski shops there sell kit rated for actual mountain cold, at similar money to India and considerably better quality. Budget €150–220 in October.

The exception, and it matters: the waterproof shell and the fleece must come with you. Iceland is 25 September. You will not have seen Grenoble yet.

A content note, since the arc runs all semester. You will be on camera for fourteen episodes. Packing inside a tight, dark colour palette means every reel is visually on-brand without you thinking about it, and the grid coheres — which was one of the fixes your Instagram audit already flagged. Two or three base colours, nothing with loud logos.

§ — The kitchen question

Skip the cooker. French supermarkets already solved it.

You asked what's better than carrying a pressure cooker. The answer is that you barely need one in France, for a reason nobody mentions: French supermarkets sell pre-cooked lentils, chickpeas and kidney beans in tins and vacuum packs, everywhere, for €1–2.

A pressure cooker exists to make dried pulses edible in fifteen minutes instead of ninety. If the pulses arrive already cooked, that entire problem — and 1.8 kg of cast aluminium — disappears. You open, drain, and season, which is where the flavour was always coming from anyway.

And the weight is worth more elsewhere

Dropping the cooker and a full utensil set frees about 2.8 kg. Run it through the same test as everything else in this bag: that weight carried as RTE into Iceland returns roughly €230. The cooker saves you maybe forty minutes a week in a kitchen you'll cook in perhaps three nights out of seven.

It isn't close. Leave it.

Do not carry — buy there

  • Pots and pans. Almost every furnished flat and residence already has them, including the Airbnb you looked at. Ask the landlord what's in the kitchen before packing a single item — this one question can save two kilos.
  • A tawa. France is a country built on crêpes; a crêpe pan is a tawa with a French accent, costs about €8, and is available in every supermarket.
  • Knives, chopping board, ladle, sieve. Action or Gifi will sell you the whole starter kit for €20–25. Knives also can't fly in cabin, so they'd eat checked weight.
  • Rice, atta, dal in bulk. Indian grocers exist in Grenoble and certainly in Lyon, which is free on your rail pass. Carry spices, buy staples — that's the whole rule.
