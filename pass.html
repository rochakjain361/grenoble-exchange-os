§ — The mechanism

Two cost structures that behave in opposite directions

European rail fares are yield-managed exactly like airline seats. A Paris–Strasbourg TGV is about €29 as an advance fare and north of €100 walking up on the day. A rail pass inverts that entirely: you pay everything upfront and each additional journey then costs nothing.

§ — The crossover

Booking early makes the pass look worse, not better

This is the counter-intuitive part, and it is the direct answer to your question. Booking early is precisely what makes ordinary tickets cheap. Your pass costs €871 whether you plan like an air-traffic controller or decide on a Thursday — so the better you plan, the worse the pass looks.

The number that answers it

The crossover sits at about 23 days. If you could reliably commit to every journey more than three weeks out, point-to-point tickets would beat the pass — by nearly €300 at the disciplined extreme. Book inside three weeks, which is what actually happens once a group chat and a timetable are involved, and the pass pulls ahead fast: €205 up at a fortnight, €505 at a week, €905 at two days.

So the honest framing is not that you bought cheap travel. You bought the right to decide late — and then to keep deciding late, all semester, at no penalty. For someone building an itinerary around eleven weekends, a shifting class schedule and whoever else is free that Friday, that option is worth more than the fare arithmetic suggests.

§ — What "early" means once you hold a pass

You now book early for seats, not for savings

Here is the substitution most pass-holders never make consciously. As a ticket-buyer, booking early saves money. As a pass-holder, booking early buys availability — the price was already settled the day you bought the pass.

  • Reservation fees don't inflate. A TGV reservation is €10–20 whether you book it in August or the night before. There is no yield curve on it.
  • But pass-holder quotas are small and fixed. Each train releases a limited number of seats to pass-holders, separate from ordinary inventory. When those go, the train is simply closed to you — at any price. You are not paying more; you are not travelling.
  • So lateness converts from a money risk into a routing risk. Miss the quota on the Friday-evening TGV to Barcelona and the trip doesn't cost more, it becomes a 6am Saturday departure or a slower route through Montpellier — you pay in weekend, not euros.
  • The rule that follows: reserve the moment you decide the weekend, not the week before it. It costs the same either way, so there is no reason whatsoever to wait — and every reason not to. This is the single cheapest discipline available to you all semester.
  • Nightjet couchettes are the extreme case — tiny quotas, and they save you a hotel night as well as the journey. If you want Venice or Vienna overnight, those book earliest of all.

The nine days in Iceland — a country with no railway at all — quietly burn €86 of pass you cannot use. Nothing to be done about that, but it explains why the answer to "should I take the train to Lyon again?" is always yes.

Your position, stated plainly

The €871 is sunk. It is gone whatever you do next, which means the only rational question left is how much value you extract before 10 December. The planned itinerary returns €904 — about 104% of cost, a rounding error from break-even.

Every marginal trip from here is nearly pure recovery. A Sunday in Annecy is €28 back for a €0 fare. Ten such days across the term is €280, which turns a 104% return into roughly 136%. The rational behaviour after buying a pass is to travel more than you otherwise would — not because travel became cheaper, but because for you it is now free at the margin.

And do Switzerland. It is the one country combining Europe's highest point-to-point fares with no reservation requirement, so a single Zurich–Bern–Interlaken weekend returns €120 against a €0 marginal cost. Nothing else on your calendar pays like it.
