Verdict

Take the central belt. It costs roughly a third as much, covers twice the countries, and — the real argument — it is the one region where a Eurail pass behaves like the thing it's sold as. In Germany, Austria, Hungary and Czechia you board without paying anything further. In Norway and Sweden you'd pay a reservation fee on nearly every leg, which is renting a pass you already own.

The Nordics are also the wrong season. Late September is past the midnight sun and before reliable aurora — and Iceland already delivers the Nordic experience, better, in Phase 2. Doing both is paying twice for one thing.

Iceland has no passenger railway at all. Nine days of your pass burn unused — unavoidable, but plan around it.

  • Find three other people before you book anything. Car, fuel and a guesthouse room all divide by four. Solo Iceland for 9 days is €1,800+; shared four ways it's €900–1,100. The GEM exchange cohort and the Indian student groups are full of people doing the same window — ask in September, not October.
  • Campervan vs car+guesthouse. A campervan folds bed and transport into one line (~€110–150/day for 4) and lets you cook — which for you is a food solution, not just a saving. Late September is the last workable month for it before the weather turns.
  • Book the car before the flight. Icelandic rental stock is small and September is still shoulder season demand. Beds you can find late; a 4×4 you cannot.
  • Aurora is live from late September — you're inside the season, at its very start. Dark hours are still short, so prioritise nights outside Reykjavík's light dome.
  • Exit KEF → CDG on 3 or 4 Oct (Icelandair and Play both fly it), collect the stored bag in Paris, TGV to Grenoble. That routing is why Paris is the right storage city.

Realistic 9-day Iceland, shared four ways, cooking most meals: €950–1,250 all-in excluding the GDN→KEF and KEF→CDG flights (≈€90 and ≈€110 respectively if booked now).

Before you book any of this

Three things could invalidate the whole window

The course dates above are last year's. They're a reliable guide to structure — that GEM teaches in staggered blocks — but not to your 2026 dates. Get your real timetable and check one thing above all: is any intensive block running 7–15 November? If one is, the window moves.

Check the attendance rule per course. French business schools commonly cap absences at one or two per course, and exceeding it can fail you outright — a grade that then transfers back to IIMB. Three absences spread across five courses is usually fine; three in one course usually isn't.

Tell the professors in September, not November. Advance notice is treated very differently from a retroactive excuse, and a professor who knows in advance will often hand you the material, move a deadline, or let you present early. Ask before you've booked, so they're granting a request rather than ratifying a decision.

Buy in Grenoble — do not waste outbound kilos

Grenoble is an alpine sports city. Decathlon is French, and Grenoble is close to the best place on earth to buy mountain kit. Carrying a winter jacket from India is three kilos spent on a worse product.

The allocation

Iceland gets the food first, and it isn't close. A kilo of RTE carried into Iceland returns about €83; the same kilo carried for the Grenoble term returns about €10, because in Grenoble you can simply buy the identical pouch for €4 or cook from scratch. Carrying 74 days of food would cost 22 kg — two-thirds of your entire allowance — to save less than €200.

So: 18 packs for Iceland (two a day, ~5.4 kg), 10 for the belt (one a day as the fallback when a city defeats you, ~3 kg), and a token 2 kg for the first week in Grenoble before you've found the grocers and bought a cooker's worth of staples. Nine kilos, spent where scarcity actually is.

The khakhra and thepla do separate work: they need no kitchen, no heating and no negotiation, which makes them the right food for travel days — the 6-hour Kraków→Gdańsk leg, the Ring Road drive between towns. Budget them by travel days, not by meals.

  • Learn the three sentences in French now — « Sans oignon, sans ail, sans pommes de terre ». Not knowing them costs you a meal a week for three months.
  • Customs: the EU bans meat and dairy from outside the union. Sealed vegetarian RTE and spices are fine — but anything with milk solids or ghee is technically dairy. Check the labels on your pouches and leave the ghee-based ones at home. Keep everything factory-sealed and declared if asked.
  • Grenoble grocers — find the Indian and Sri Lankan shops in your first week, not your fourth. Lyon's are larger and 1h15 away; make one bulk run in October and you're set until December.

Money

  • Scapia is the right primary — zero forex markup on a Visa network is genuinely the best card you can be carrying. But it is a credit card, and that creates three failure modes worth pre-empting: some European fuel pumps and rail kiosks pre-authorise amounts that trip limits; a handful of small merchants in Poland and Czechia are debit-or-cash; and ATM withdrawal on a credit card is expensive everywhere.
  • Carry a second, unrelated card — a forex debit card (Niyo, Fi, IndusInd) on a different network where possible. The scenario you're insuring against isn't cost, it's a single card being blocked on a Sunday in Gdańsk.
  • Always choose to be charged in the local currency, never in rupees. Dynamic currency conversion at the terminal quietly adds 3–5% and it silently cancels the entire benefit of holding Scapia.
  • Don't buy złoty, koruna, forint or króna in India. You'll take a terrible rate twice. Withdraw locally from a bank ATM — and decline the machine's own conversion offer.
  • Iceland is effectively cashless — card works at petrol pumps, campsites, everywhere. Carry almost no krónur.
  • Tell your banks the travel dates before you fly, and check whether your remittances fall under the education TCS rate rather than the general one — the difference is material on semester-sized sums. Worth one conversation with your bank, not a guess.

Connectivity

  • Phase 1 + Iceland (11 Sep – 4 Oct): a regional eSIM. Airalo or Nomad, Europe-wide, ~€20–30 for 20 GB. It activates before you land and works across all seven countries plus Iceland with no shop visits while you're moving daily. Do not use Indian roaming — it will cost more than the flight's meal service.
  • From October: a French line. Free Mobile at €19.99/month is unlimited in France with generous EU roaming — which matters, because it covers your weekend trips too. Bouygues and SFR are comparable.
  • The catch: French postpaid usually wants a French address and a RIB (bank details), and a French bank account wants an address — a loop that catches every exchange student. The way through is a prepaid SIM (Lycamobile, Lebara) on day one with just your passport, then switch to Free once the address and account exist.
  • Keep your Indian number alive. ₹200–300/month of minimum recharge. Every Indian bank OTP, your Scapia login, DigiLocker and UPI are welded to it. Losing that number mid-semester is the single most disruptive small failure available to you.

§ 09 — What the pass actually buys

Grenoble is a spur, not a hub — and the pass has an expiry

Two structural facts shape every weekend of Phase 3. Neither is a problem if you know them in October; both are expensive surprises in December.

Confirmed: 3-month continuous. That's the better pass to hold — unlimited boardings, no day-rationing, no reason ever to think twice about a train. But "continuous" buys unlimited travel, not unlimited free travel, and the gap between those two is where pass-holders get caught.

The one real cost

Reservations are not covered, and on French high-speed they're compulsory

Every TGV, Frecciarossa, AVE, Eurostar and Nightjet requires a paid seat reservation on top of the pass, with a small pass-holder quota that sells out weeks ahead. You cannot walk onto a TGV with a Eurail pass and a hopeful expression.

Budget €250–350 across the semester for reservations. It's the true cost of your "free" travel — and the reason to book each Friday train the day you decide the weekend, not the Wednesday before it.

What continuous actually unlocks

Your local radius becomes genuinely free and spontaneous. French regional trains — TER — need no reservation at all. Grenoble to Lyon, Chambéry, Annecy, Valence and Grenoble's whole valley is board-and-sit, zero cost, zero planning, as many times as you like. Treat Lyon as a second living room: the Indian grocers, the airport, concerts, a Tuesday evening if you feel like it.

Switzerland is the quiet bonus — Swiss trains almost never require reservations, so Zurich, Bern and Interlaken cost you nothing to reach even though they're 4–5h out. The expensive part of Switzerland is standing in it, not getting there.

So the strategy inverts from "how many trips can I afford" to "how many Fridays are there". Eleven. Use all of them.

Hard cliff · around 10 December

Activate 11 Sep and the pass dies before your last two windows

Three continuous months from 11 September expires ~10 December. That covers Phase 1, Iceland (useless there — no railway) and windows G01–G09. It does not cover G10, G11, or your run up to Paris on 21 December, which is precisely when French fares peak for Christmas.

Therefore: front-load. Every destination that needs a long, reservation-heavy haul — Venice, Barcelona, Nice, Florence, Paris — gets done in October and November while the pass is alive. December is for skiing (bus, not rail) and Lyon. And buy the 21 Dec Grenoble→Paris TGV separately, now, at ~€60–100 — it will only get worse.
